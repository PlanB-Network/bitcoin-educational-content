# Democracy and Liberty

We are told that we live in democracies and that we are therefore free. But do we actually govern as a people — and is democracy really the same thing as liberty? Building on the realist Machiavellian school of political thought, this course shows that every society is ruled by an organized minority, examines how public opinion is both the true foundation of power and the object of constant engineering, and traces how modern representative democracy undermines itself both politically and economically — with fiat money as its financial engine.

The course then proposes another definition of democracy, much closer to freedom: political self-determination and the free market — and closes with a concrete program, from decentralization to sound money. Fifteen lectures to see the political world with new eyes.

## Democracy and Liberty: Asking the Right Questions

<chapterId>10b633f0-7526-4e74-b958-0d1114eac7b0</chapterId>

In the West, few words carry more weight than *democracy* and *liberty*. They anchor our political culture in a way they simply do not in Asia, where the emphasis falls instead on the collective, on harmony, on peace. Yet not everything is right with our democracies — and any honest investigation must start by asking questions.

### Six questions worth asking

1. **Do we actually govern as a people?** We are told that we do. Is it the case?
2. **Why do modern democracies seem to be in crisis**, both politically and economically — at home and in international relations? Is this connected to democracy itself, or merely happening in parallel?
3. **Why do parties across the spectrum tend toward state interventionism?** Compare today's party programs with those of the 1980s–90s: the Cold War era offered genuinely ideological choice — communists arguing for nationalizing the means of production, classical liberals arguing for dismantling the welfare state. Today the labels "left" and "right" attach to issues like immigration rather than to the essential question: should the state play a large role in society, or a small one? If left and right are defined by that question, they remain relevant — but the real choice offered to voters has narrowed.
4. **Why does state debt keep rising?** Across the West, debt as a share of GDP has climbed almost constantly since the beginning of the 20th century — in parallel with the arrival of democracy. Is there a connection?
5. **What about fiat money?** Our money is not based on real value but on government decree: legal tender we must accept. What role do central banks play, and how does this shape society?
6. **What is the connection between democracy and liberty?** We tend to use the two terms almost interchangeably: we are in democracies, therefore we are free. But they are quite different concepts. China, officially a communist dictatorship, allows considerable economic liberty. Meanwhile in the UK, France, or Germany — democracies all — freedom of expression and economic freedom are being reduced, sometimes fast, sometimes progressively. We cannot be so quick to equate the two.

This course tries to answer these questions.

### The agenda

1. **Introduction** — this chapter: definitions and background.
2. **How societies are ruled** — before we can discuss democracy, we must understand how *any* society actually functions in terms of power.
3. **Characteristics of democracy** — its origins and its mechanics.
4. **The failure of democracy** — the ways democracy is failing, which are not always evident.
5. **Real democracy** — another definition, much closer to freedom.
6. **What is to be done** — once the problem is identified, what can be done about it.

Parts 2 through 4 identify the problem; parts 5 and 6 propose the way out.

### Defining democracy

*Democracy* comes from the Greek — *demos*, people-rule or people-power. But which people? Surely not all of them: at best a majority, which immediately excludes some. Formally: **a form of government in which sovereign power resides in the people as a whole and is exercised either directly by them (direct democracy) or by officials elected by them (representative democracy)**.

The definition already contains a tension: sovereign power "in the people as a whole", yet in practice the basic rule of the majority. We will return to this.

### Defining freedom

The definition used throughout this course: **freedom as the absence of coercion**. Seen this way, democracy is obviously not the same thing as freedom.

The French classical liberal **Benjamin Constant** distinguished two freedoms:

- **The freedom of the ancients** — political in nature: the citizen of ancient Greece is free *because he votes*. Under this definition, freedom and democracy nearly coincide.
- **The freedom of the moderns** — personal and economic: the ability to make one's own choices in one's private life and economy. This is the freedom people actually value today.

Three clarifications:

1. **Freedom here is an external concept** — not a psychological or emotional state. One may *feel* free in any circumstances; that is not the subject of a political discussion.
2. **Not all coercion counts.** Gravity constrains you; a wall stops you; biology ages you. Nobody calls that unfreedom. What matters politically is coercion arising from human organization.
3. **The relevant coercion is state coercion** — economic, legal, political. Freedom, for this course, is a political concept: it concerns life in society.

While democracy often *aims* to protect freedom, it is no guarantee: democratic systems also infringe, sometimes significantly, on individual liberties. Taxation is the everyday example — you may accept it as part of a social contract, but it is imposed, and refusing it leads to prison. Defenders of democracy will even argue such infringements are *necessary* for the system to work. That trade-off deserves scrutiny, not assumption.

### Why society comes first

Democracy and freedom exist *in society*. It is easy to jump straight to arguing about what is good or bad in a political system — but we must first step back and look at how human beings actually live with, and rule over, each other. That is the subject of Part 2.

### A 200-year-old warning

**Alexis de Tocqueville**, the French classical liberal who traveled the early American republic and wrote *Democracy in America*, put it plainly:

> "I dearly love liberty and respect for rights — but not democracy."

Two centuries ago, a great thinker already understood the distinction this course explores. Throughout these chapters you will find quotations that are sometimes hundreds of years old — deliberately. These ideas are not new; they have been expressed for centuries, and then lost — through our media, our universities, and a state-organized education system whose biases should surprise no one. Part of the work is simply rediscovering them.

# How Societies Are Ruled

<partId>044d91ee-deb3-4154-8548-b24583a48c96</partId>


## The Machiavellian School

<chapterId>410c4ba2-267f-468e-a3a5-9be2788f0e3d</chapterId>

Before we can talk about political systems, we must clarify how societies are actually ruled. Fortunately, a school of thought focused on precisely this point: the **Machiavellians**. They are not well known today — and there may be reasons for that — but they are among the most important political thinkers of the modern era, and much of this course builds on them.

### A realist approach to politics

The Machiavellian tradition is, above all, a **realist** approach to politics. That sounds obvious — of course one should be realistic about politics — but most people are not. Politics is commonly approached emotionally, ideologically, or simply without study. Politicians themselves rarely present politics realistically in public: doing so would give the game away.

The tradition goes back to **Niccolò Machiavelli**, the first major political thinker since Aristotle to treat politics as a *science* — cold-bloodedly, without ideology or wishful thinking, asking only what serves the ruler and what serves the ruled. *The Prince* is famous for its advice; what matters here is its method.

### The Machiavellian thinkers

Machiavelli inspired a group of late-19th / early-20th century thinkers we group together as the Machiavellians:

- **Gaetano Mosca** (Italian) — author of *The Ruling Class*
- **Robert Michels** (German, lived largely in Italy) — author of *Political Parties* (1911)
- **Vilfredo Pareto** (Italian) — also the statistician of "Pareto efficiency" fame
- **Georges Sorel** (French)

What they share: a **rejection of idealistic and ideological views of politics**. The idealist analyzes society as he wants it to be, which leads to wrong conclusions. The Machiavellians analyze society *as it is*, using history — you must know history well to understand the present. Their focus: **power dynamics and elite rule**.

Their Latin roots matter. These thinkers came from — or lived in — Italy and France, cultures with a certain cynicism about political progress: things are as they are because human nature is what it is. This contrasts sharply with the Anglo-Saxon optimism that dominates political thinking today, forever in search of the better society. The Machiavellian lens is sometimes called conservative — fair enough, since it assumes the same political problems recur from antiquity through the Middle Ages to today, because human nature approaches politics the same way in every era. Some have even called it fascist; that is a misunderstanding, and sometimes intentional smearing designed to keep these thinkers unread.

Notably, the key works — Mosca's *Ruling Class*, Michels' *Political Parties*, and the book recommended most strongly here, **James Burnham's *The Machiavellians* (1943)**, an excellent review of the whole school by the American conservative thinker — have seen remarkably few editions and remain obscure in mainstream discourse.

Burnham summarizes the school in two commitments:

1. They **"refused to take at face value the words, beliefs, and ideals of men."** Permanent skepticism — especially toward political speech. What is said always hides something that is not said.
2. **The subject matter of politics is the struggle for social power** — regardless of the political regime. The struggle is human and permanent; it does not disappear because we have democracy.

### The iron law of oligarchy

The Machiavellians' most famous concept. Observe human beings in any group: some lead, some are led, most are somewhere in between. You can see it as early as the schoolyard. And — a point about human nature that democracies especially dislike admitting — **most people accept being led, provided the rule is tolerable**. "Tolerable" is subjective and shifts with the times, which is why rulers must constantly attend to the opinions of the ruled: the crowd is fickle.

**Robert Michels** demonstrated the law empirically. Studying Germany's SPD — the largest political party in the country, and one *formally committed* to internal democracy — he showed that as organizations grow, decision-making inevitably shifts to a small minority whose interests progressively diverge from the majority's. Even a party that proclaims collective decision-making ends up run by a few. The pattern holds for all organizations.

### Who rises in organizations?

Those who are *serious about the struggle for power*. They rise on character: ambition, ruthlessness, thick skin, eloquence — interpersonal and political skills, visible from early in life.

This exposes a paradox that buries **Plato's philosopher-king**. If the traits that carry people to the top of organizations are political skills, then those who rise are *not* necessarily those with the best or deepest ideas for society. The wisest ruling is a theoretical ideal — and if there is one criticism to make of Plato, with his ideal forms, it is precisely that he was an idealist.

### Public vs. private hierarchy

Everything said so far applies to a private company as much as to the state. But there is one important difference: **commercial organizations openly acknowledge their hierarchy**. In a corporation, who directs and who executes is explicit and transparent — no secrecy. **Political organizations mask their hierarchy behind democratic myths**: they proclaim collective decision-making while a small group decides. That gap between the official story and reality creates a permanent stress inside political organizations — the lower ranks must constantly be convinced that the organization is democratic.

Keep this asymmetry in mind. It returns throughout the course.

## The Ruling Minority and the Ruled Majority

<chapterId>32adb00b-f28a-4a1a-b999-0b693aad6c83</chapterId>

The key message of the Machiavellians: **every society is composed of a ruling minority and a ruled majority.** This chapter examines that claim — and the minority itself.

### An old idea

Is the rule of the many — the very basis of democracy — against nature? Thinkers have long said yes.

- **Aristotle** equated mass rule with mob rule: *ochlocracy*, the rule of chaos, where law and order break down. This was emphatically not what the Greeks meant by democracy, which was ordered and involved a limited part of the population.
- **Rousseau**, *The Social Contract* (1762): "It is against the natural order that the many should govern and the few should be governed."
- **David Hume**, the Scottish Enlightenment philosopher: "Nothing appears more surprising to those who consider human affairs with a philosophical eye than the easiness with which the many are governed by the few, and the implicit submission with which men resign their own sentiments and passions to those of their rulers."

Hume's "implicit submission" echoes what we saw earlier: most people agree to be led, if the rule is tolerable.

The Machiavellians formalized this into a law. **James Burnham**:

> "The existence of a minority ruling class is, it must be stressed, a universal feature of all organized societies of which we have any record. It holds no matter what the social and political forms — whether the society is feudal or capitalist or slave or collectivist or monarchical or oligarchical or democratic; no matter what the constitutions and laws; no matter what the professions and beliefs."

### How can a minority rule?

A ruling minority is typically a few percent of the population. It rules because it is **motivated, organized, and informed** — never underestimate what motivation does to human potential — while the ruled majority is **large but disorganized and uninformed**, especially about political matters, and *consents* to be ruled (Hume's implicit submission; Mosca's *The Ruling Class* develops this).

This holds regardless of the political system — which creates an inherent tension **within democracy specifically**: permanent minority (oligarchic) rule, often ruled by money and influence, clashing with the myth of popular sovereignty. If your whole society is built on the idea that the people rule, and a minority in fact rules, significant tensions follow. That goes a long way toward explaining the instability of Western political systems today.

### Primitive vs. complex societies

The ruling minority looks different depending on the society:

| | Primitive society | Complex society |
|---|---|---|
| Size & shape | Small group (tens of families), homogeneous, ethnically and culturally similar to the majority | Multi-layered: different groups with different kinds and degrees of influence |
| Visibility | Open, accessible — the bigger house down the street | Partially hidden, from the majority *and* between its own layers; outsiders can lay the puzzle but never hold all the pieces |
| Interests | Practical, down-to-earth, largely shared with the majority | Heavily financial, ideological, uprooted from the majority |
| Horizon | Short-term goals | Long-term, abstract, lofty ambitions |

The more the minority differs from the majority, the more tension between the two. Complex societies produce distant, ideological elites — and the tensions to match.

### Who is the Western ruling minority?

Concretely, in today's West:

- **Banking families** — the Rothschilds are the famous case, but many families built comparable fortunes from the 19th century onward
- **Financial institutions** — major banks and asset managers: BlackRock, Vanguard, Goldman Sachs, HSBC, Citigroup
- **The UN orbit** — international NGOs, foundations and think tanks that gravitate around the United Nations (the WEF, the Rockefeller Foundation and similar), often financed by the same financial institutions and families
- **Leadership of multinational corporations** — largely owned by those same institutions, who therefore have a say in who rises; you do not reach the highest echelons without broadly aligning with the owners' interests
- **Western governments** — with a caveat: the political leaders are the *faces*, the spokespersons, not necessarily the most powerful members of the minority. The highest civil servants, working in the shadows, may influence policy and strategic direction far more than the leaders seen on TV.

### The circulation of elites

Individuals change faster than direction. A complex society's ruling minority, with its long-term and ideological goals, changes *course* slowly — but its *members* change constantly: conditions shift, challengers arrive, people age out.

This is the Machiavellian concept of the **circulation of elites**, and it is how a ruling minority avoids decay. In *The Leopard* (*Il Gattopardo*), Lampedusa's prince says it perfectly: **"Everything must change for everything to remain the same."** Change the people, if necessary, to maintain the structure of power.

Burnham: the circulation of elites is *a safeguard against bureaucratic degeneration, a check on errors, and a protection against revolution*. A young, fresh ruling minority constantly absorbs able newcomers with newer ideas. The British upper class is the standout historical example — more successful than most national elites at integrating up-and-coming outsiders: entrepreneurs, foreigners, self-made men from academia and business.

When a ruling minority decays — becomes incompetent, mismanages wealth — a newer, cleverer section of the elite replaces it. The replacement can be violent (revolution) or smooth (reform); done cleverly enough, the majority hardly notices. And note carefully: **revolutions are never organized and driven by the majority.** That is the myth. They are always led by a dissident part of the ruling minority, which may or may not enjoy majority support. The French Revolution is a textbook case.

Read the U.S. Declaration of Independence in this light — "whenever any form of government becomes destructive of these ends, it is the right of the people to alter or to abolish it" — and you see utopian wording. The people themselves never alter or abolish a government. They can pressure, and they can support a section of the ruling minority that does.

## The Crucial Role of Public Opinion

<chapterId>a1cc9f19-3a4d-44b0-9991-acce2de7c568</chapterId>

The previous chapters may have left a bleak impression: if every society is ruled by a minority and nothing can change that, what hope is there for freedom? But the picture is incomplete. There are connections between the minority and the majority that cannot be underestimated — and the strongest of them is **public opinion**.

### All power rests on opinion

However strong a ruling minority is, it must take the majority's opinion into account — because **all power of a ruling minority ultimately rests on public opinion**.

The classic statement is **Étienne de La Boétie's *Discourse on Voluntary Servitude*** (16th century; La Boétie, a friend of Montaigne, lived 1530–1563). His insight: people *voluntarily* accept servitude — most do not want the responsibility and effort of ruling — but they want to be ruled *well*. When they are not, they express dissatisfaction. So it is in the ruling minority's own survival interest to heed, to some extent, the wishes of the ruled.

> "There is no need to fight the tyrant, nor even to defend oneself from him. He is defeated by himself if the country does not consent to servitude. It is not a question of taking anything from him, but only of giving him nothing."

Even the tyrant depends on the majority not disliking him too much. And what holds for the dictator holds doubly for the democratically elected leader, who is *officially* obliged to consider the wishes of those who voted for him. In this light, representative democracy is relatively natural — *as long as* representatives largely fulfill the wishes of those they represent.

### Mosca's formula

If all power rests on opinion, then the ruling minority must **justify** its power in the majority's eyes. This is another key Machiavellian contribution — **the political formula**. Burnham:

> "The ruling minority always seeks to justify and legitimize its rule in part through a formula, without which the social structure would disintegrate."

Historically the formula was the **divine right of kings** — the rulers' special closeness to God — which served for thousands of years and died with the French Revolution and the 19th-century collapse of monarchical-aristocratic regimes. As populations grew more educated and stopped believing some men are closer to God than others, the formula had to be replaced.

Its replacement is even better: **"I am the people's representative."** How can I not be in power, if I am merely the vehicle through which the people expresses its wishes? People-power is the formula that justifies rule in every modern democratic system.

### Improving opinion vs. influencing it

Since opinion is the foundation of power, the minority wants to control it. There are exactly two ways:

1. **Improve public opinion** — govern well, implement the program one was elected on. Opinion follows merit.
2. **Influence public opinion** — propaganda, censorship, disinformation: shape the opinion directly.

Western ruling minorities use both. But note the dynamic: as a ruling minority grows more detached from the majority (the complex-society drift from the previous chapter), *improving* opinion becomes harder — their interests diverge too much — so the effort shifts toward *influencing* it. And democracies have a special need for opinion-control, because their very formula is people's rule: the polls, surveys and permanent mood-tracking that every government and party runs, election year or not, are the clearest sign that public opinion is the key variable of democratic power.

### A short history of engineered opinion

- **World War I** — the first massive modern propaganda program: in about two years, Americans went from broadly sympathetic to Germany to firmly anti-German and pro-British, carrying Wilson into the war in 1917. (Recall that the U.S. and Britain had been antagonists through most of the 19th century.) Proof that a government could rapidly and massively reshape opinion.
- **Edward Bernays** — the father of propaganda, whose major works (*Crystallizing Public Opinion*, *Propaganda*) date from the 1920s. Bernays worked business as well as politics: the smoking women of Hollywood's golden era were a conscious campaign to open the female market for tobacco companies. And to be fair to him and his successors: they were not simply evil manipulators. Many believed, naively or realistically, that society *needs* directing for its own good — that mass opinion is scattered raw material waiting to be shaped. (Think of Steve Jobs: don't ask what people want; provide what they will like.) Condescending toward the majority, certainly. Entirely false, perhaps not.

The domination this enables goes far beyond politics and law: it is domination through ideas, feelings, and emotions. Of the two great dystopias, Orwell's *1984* and **Huxley's *Brave New World***, it is Huxley's that matters here — rule by getting the majority to *love* the society it is not free in. In one of his final interviews (circa 1960, easily found online), Huxley explained that new communication methods would let governments control opinion far better than ever before.

Bernays himself, *Propaganda* (1928):

> "The conscious and intelligent manipulation of the organized habits and opinions of the masses is an important element in democratic society. Those who manipulate this unseen mechanism of society constitute an invisible government which is the true ruling power of our country. We are governed, our minds are molded, our tastes formed, our ideas suggested, largely by men we have never heard of. […] It is they who pull the wires which control the public mind."

Truer today than in 1928. Unless we take the trouble to read and think for ourselves, most of our ideas will have been whispered to us by the ruling minority and those who serve it.

### The key works

A century of scholarship on engineered opinion, in one ladder:

| Work | Idea |
|---|---|
| Gustave Le Bon, *The Crowd* (1895) | Crowds are irrational and easily manipulated |
| Gaetano Mosca, *The Ruling Class* | Elites justify their rule through myths — the political formula |
| Edward Bernays, *Propaganda* (1928) | Public opinion is engineered by elites |
| Aldous Huxley, *Brave New World* (1932) | Rule through *pleasing* servitude |
| Friedrich Hayek, "The Intellectuals and Socialism" | The "second-hand dealers in ideas" disseminate what the minority wants adopted |
| Chomsky & Herman, *Manufacturing Consent* (1988) | Media serves elite interests — mostly unconsciously, without coercion; journalists are not part of the ruling minority, yet serve it |
| Mattias Desmet, mass formation | Collective delusions in crisis — wars and the COVID period as windows of maximal manipulability |
| Timur Kuran, *Private Truths, Public Lies* (1990s) | We lie in public for social peace — conforming to accepted opinion while telling the truth only to family and close friends |

With that, the groundwork on how societies are ruled is complete. Next: democracy itself, beginning with its origins.

## Origins: Athens and Direct Democracy

<chapterId>9f8961e8-03d0-4366-8725-968a13e5327c</chapterId>

With the theoretical background in place — minority rule, the circulation of elites, the power of public opinion — we can now turn to democracy itself. First: its origins, in Athens.

### Why Athens?

Athens was the city-state that implemented democracy for the first time, and its influence on the political development of the West is enormous. To understand what democracy was there — and how different it is from what we call democracy today — is to understand where the concept comes from.

### How Athenian democracy worked

- It began with early reforms by leaders like **Solon** (6th century BC), who reduced the power of the aristocrats and expanded political participation for common citizens.
- **Cleisthenes** then reorganized the Athenian political system, laying democracy's foundations: a council, and **ten tribes based on residence rather than aristocratic family ties**. Residence matters — it determines where you live, where you pay taxes, and which laws affect you. Rights and duties, tied to place: already an important political concept.
- The institutions allowed citizens to **participate directly** in decision-making — not through representatives. Some 30,000, later 60,000, Athenians could participate: they gathered physically on the square, deliberated, and voted.

### Rights — and duties

The crucial cultural difference: Greek citizens were **expected to actively participate in public life**. They had rights, but also duties — including the duty to *be informed* about what they would vote on.

Compare today: we vote for representatives, and let *them* be the informed ones. Convenient — and dangerous, as we shall see. Athenian society was far more politicized: political freedom meant the capability of engaging in the *polis*. Today, political life concerns only a few, while most people live their economic lives and ask mainly for economic freedom.

This is Benjamin Constant's distinction again, made explicit in his famous 1819 speech: **the freedom of the ancients** (political participation, with heavily regulated private life) versus **the freedom of the moderns** (personal and economic independence). For Aristotle, democracy was an expression of freedom — but freedom in the ancient, political sense, not the classical-liberal sense of absence of coercion. And remember: for the Greeks democracy was never *mass* rule — that was ochlocracy, mob rule, chaos. It was always a limited part of the population, voting directly.

### Recurrence: the Greek theory of political cycles

Greek thought did not see time as a straight line. Political regimes inevitably transform into one another. In Plato's cycle: **aristocracy** decays into **oligarchy** (rule of money), which transforms into **democracy** (public opinion overthrows the oligarchs), which decays into **tyranny** — and back to aristocracy.

There is real truth in this — and it collides with our modern "end of history" complacency, the assumption that liberal democracy will simply continue until the end of time. Nobody today seriously considers the end of democracy or its transformation into another regime, which leaves us dangerously unprepared for the technical and demographic changes that inevitably carry political consequences. (Modern echoes of recurrence exist — the "Fourth Turning", Spengler — but the idea remains rare.) Keep the cycle in mind; it will return throughout the course.

### The question of size

Athens was a big city — but a city. Athenian democracy was never tried at the scale of a nation of millions. What the Athenians built fit their size, and fit their culture of civic duty. Our democracies differ on both counts — yet Athenian democracy shaped all early thinking about democracy in Western Europe and in the founding of the United States.

Next: what happened when democracy *was* tried at the scale of nations — representative democracy, and why it can fairly be called an illusion.

## The Illusion of Representative Democracy

<chapterId>c3e33d26-4a66-4f23-98f8-0f6637f22ff8</chapterId>

We now arrive at the heart of the course. Representative democracy doesn't really work — not that it doesn't work *at all*, but its flaws are significant, structural, and not obvious. They only become visible in the light of the Machiavellian concepts introduced earlier.

### From Athens to representation

Until the 18th century, democracy remained a largely theoretical concept modeled on Athens — there was very little democracy anywhere (republican Florence, periods in the Netherlands, little else). Even theorists saw the problem of scale. **Rousseau**, in *The Social Contract*:

> "It is inconceivable that the people should remain incessantly assembled to attend to public affairs."

Early 19th-century experiments confirmed it in practice: given the size of nations and populations, and the communications of the era, direct democracy at national level was impractical. **Democratic representation emerged only when direct democracy proved impractical** — it is a workaround, not an ideal.

Could technology change that today? Perhaps — the communications exist. But as we will see, technology was never the only obstacle.

### Representation institutionalizes minority rule

Accepting political representation carries a danger: it **institutionalizes minority rule**. It is voluntary servitude in La Boétie's sense, wearing a democratic coat. The paradox: we say the people rule through elected representatives — but what do those representatives do between elections? How independent are they once in the capital? How are they influenced? In theory they vote according to their constituencies or their program. Can we be sure?

Look realistically — without rosy spectacles — and the conclusion is automatic: **there are entire policy domains in which the majority is never involved at all**: foreign policy, monetary policy, defense, immigration, among others. These decisions are the exclusive domain of the ruling minority. Most never appear in any electoral program; many are not even communicated after the fact. Open the news any day and watch decisions being made without the voters ever being consulted.

And here is the uncomfortable part: this seems to suit everyone. The majority does not even *expect* to decide foreign or monetary policy — it never complains about the exclusion. The voters thereby implicitly accept that their representation is partial. The ruling minority, for its part, maintains the **illusion of representative democracy** by simply never describing these domains as undemocratic — the objective being social stability, which is also, in fairness, in the majority's interest.

The system holds together through **tactical concessions**: in the areas that touch the majority directly — taxes, social services — the ruling minority is far more responsive. Politicians want to be elected; they promise and partially deliver where it is visible. The majority is not educated enough in the indirect domains (tariffs, interest rates, foreign policy) to see how substantially — if indirectly, and with delay — those decisions impact their lives.

### Bonapartism: the authoritarian tendency inside democracy

Rousseau's "general will" — a mythical will of the entire society which, frankly, does not exist — becomes a tool for justifying minority rule. Between elections, the elected legislator has free rein; Rousseau himself said that between elections the individual is a slave. (Rousseau is a divisive figure: often brilliantly prescient, often a precursor of socialism — handle with precaution.)

**Robert Michels** devoted a chapter of *Political Parties* to this phenomenon: **Bonapartism** — named for Napoleon III, elected president of France, later dictator. The mechanism: *the elected ruler personifies the majority; therefore all resistance to his will is anti-democratic*. Elected democratically, he cannot be blamed — he is merely the vessel of the sacrosanct will of the people. This is Mosca's formula (people-power replacing divine right) weaponized: elected leaders, especially by universal suffrage, can invoke the people's will to justify authoritarian measures. We see it constantly in so-called liberal democracies.

Note that Bonapartism does not require abolishing democratic institutions — though elected leaders have done exactly that (Napoleon III, Hitler, Algeria in the 1990s). It is a *style of rule*: authoritarian, justified by election.

The clearest modern illustration: **European referendums**. When a vote is meant merely to confirm the "historical necessity" of European integration, a wrong answer is not accepted: Ireland and Denmark were made to vote again after a few weeks of propaganda; France's 2005 rejection of the European Constitution was bypassed via parliamentary ratification. The people voted "the wrong way," so the vote was disregarded or repeated. *(Editorial: the transcript also cites US election-fraud claims and the Biden/Kamala substitution — professor's position, see flag.)*

### The end of the democratic ideal?

The illusion has held for a long time because of two core misconceptions held by the majority:

1. **That political representatives are loyal to voters and disinterested.** For the most part, they are not — believing otherwise is naive.
2. **That voters are informed and rational about politics.** They are not — the next chapter is devoted to this.

Democracy retains a deeply positive connotation, sustained by an education system and media with every interest in promoting it as the highest political ideal. But underneath sits a structural disenfranchisement — and in the last decade, a **new consciousness** has been emerging in the majority. Two causes:

- **Uncensored information**: for the first time in history, social media (X/Twitter, YouTube…) gives the majority easy access to political information that libraries always held but few ever fetched. (For how long is another question — a ruling minority cannot indefinitely accept uncensored information that threatens its power.)
- **Actual political deterioration**: visibly declining quality of leaders, of their education and judgment; economic and cultural stagnation.

The electoral symptoms are everywhere: Brexit, populist parties outside the traditional ruling minority rising across Europe and the United States. Scholars of the democratic process now write openly about it — Professor Michel Maffesoli of the Sorbonne: *"The end of the democratic ideal is manifesting itself."*

Next, the second misconception: the voter himself.

## The Voter

<chapterId>91e2a9ec-392d-429d-9630-65ffc4df7972</chapterId>

Of the two great misconceptions about democracy, we now examine the second: that the voter is an informed and rational actor in politics. He is not — and interestingly, that may even be rational of him.

### The unscientific approach to politics

Another Machiavellian contribution (Mosca and Michels wrote convincingly on it): the masses take an **unscientific approach to politics**. Voters act mostly emotionally and superficially — not because they are stupid, but because almost nobody invests the time to understand politics theoretically *and* practically: the programs, the candidates' real profiles, the machinery of the political process. There are exceptions — voters who know every in and out — but they are rare.

Even Shakespeare had it, in *Hamlet*: the "distracted multitude, who like not in their judgment, but their eyes." Interview politically disengaged people and they will tell you plainly: they prefer a candidate for his looks, for her perceived personality. Judging with the eyes is inevitable when one spends no time on the substance.

**James Burnham** summarized: "It is exceedingly difficult for men to be scientific or logical about social and political problems." The reason is structural: **the consequences of political decisions on our lives are indirect** — indirect in mechanism (a tariff or an interest-rate change never debits your account directly) and indirect in time (effects arrive with delay). Even intelligent, educated people struggle to reason scientifically about causes so removed from their effects. The evidence of history, Burnham notes, confirms and reconfirms it: the masses simply do not think scientifically about political and social aims.

### Didn't education fix this?

The 19th century believed universal education would enable the masses to be scientific about politics and thereby reach a perfect democracy — idealism about democracy was still alive. It did not happen, for three reasons:

1. **Universal education is provided by the state** — some messages are delivered, others are not. Its task was never to explain how politics really works; it is made to produce good taxpayers (cynically) or fulfilled contributors to society (charitably). Either way, not political scientists.
2. **Increased control of information** — public education plus propaganda counterbalanced the benefits of literacy.
3. **The culture of the expert** — modern education produces specialists, and specialists rationally ignore topics that will not advance their careers: culture, philosophy, macroeconomics, political philosophy. Compare university education of the 19th and early 20th century, broad and classical, with today's.

Add a final layer: even the educated citizen is ignorant of **state administration** — how the machine actually functions — because almost no one has theoretical or practical experience of it.

### The old warnings

These observations are centuries old — which is precisely why this course quotes old thinkers: the ideas are not new, they have been *lost*.

- **Montesquieu**, *The Spirit of the Laws* (1748): the average person lacks the *time*, the *interest*, and the *competence* for politics — a standing danger for any democratic system, since it lets representatives take the law into their own hands unwatched.
- **Benjamin Constant**: "The danger of modern liberty is that, absorbed in the enjoyment of our private independence and in the pursuit of our particular interests, we should surrender our right to share in political power too easily." The exact opposite of Athens — and the flip side of the freedom of the moderns.
- **Tocqueville**, *Democracy in America* (1835): economic life breeds indifference to political life. The Americans of the 1830s were so absorbed in personal economic success that they no longer cared about politics. This is democracy as victim of its own success: when the system works and delivers economic freedom, people disengage — *precisely* the moment when the ruling minority can decide against the majority's interests unobserved.

### Is the voter perhaps… smart?

Here is the twist. The voter's indifference is, in a sense, **rational**. The causality between national politics and one's own life is real but tenuous, distant, fuzzy. Mastering politics costs enormous effort; the reward is invisible — your life does not improve, your income does not rise, and **your vote is a drop in an ocean of ballots**. Effort where there is a clear reward is the perfectly natural human strategy, and politics does not offer one. Genuine political engagement must be driven from inside — a passion for justice or liberty — and that will never describe a majority.

So we cannot blame voters for behaving as they do. But notice what the behavior amounts to: **an implicit admission that they do not rule**. Vote once every few years, feel good about it, then leave politics alone and adapt to whatever comes — even by moving themselves or their money abroad.

The corollary: interest in politics rises when conditions truly deteriorate. That is exactly what we observe in the West today — and it is not a coincidence.

## Democracy as a Conduit for Public Opinion

<chapterId>c8bef4c2-1f95-4f38-8dfe-ab9d89313c7b</chapterId>

Not everything is negative. Democracy has one genuinely important function when it works well — and this chapter is about that function: democracy as a **conduit**, a **pressure valve**, for public opinion.

### Why democracy stays popular

Despite the contradictions we have seen — universal minority rule, the illusion of representation, mounting political tensions — the majority still resists criticizing democracy. Four reasons:

1. **People know no other system.** It is very difficult for someone who grew up in a democracy, educated to see it in a constantly positive light, to even imagine an alternative. (People who grew up behind the Iron Curtain often judge Western democracy more lucidly, having lived something else.) The same is true of money: we are so used to inflation and fiat currency that another monetary system is almost unimaginable.
2. **We are taught to value democracy above freedom.** The West's whole value system is constructed around democracy, not liberty — which is why violating freedom (high taxes, coercive rules) causes little scandal, while violating democracy, or appearing to, causes a big one.
3. **Democracy is viewed as an improvement over monarchy** — whatever its flaws, it will always look better than the monarchy that school taught us to despise. Reality is more mitigated (next section).
4. **Democracy can channel public opinion** — the most important point, developed below.

### A fair look at monarchy

The claim is that democracy is morally superior to repressive monarchies. Partly true: under many monarchies, freedom of expression and of the press were tightly controlled — Voltaire did his time in prison under the Ancien Régime, and the story repeats in Sweden *(under Gustav III — verify)* and across Europe. But it is not the whole story:

- **Minority rule is universal** — monarchy or democracy, a minority rules either way. School never teaches this.
- **Voting predates modern democracy.** The monarchical Middle Ages were full of voting schemes at many levels of society — more rigid, yes, with guilds restricting mobility, but not the vote-free darkness the textbooks imply.
- **Monarchies had a natural inclination to protect property.** Monarchy is tied to aristocratic land ownership; respect for property is its constitutional principle. Concretely: no fiat money — currencies based on gold, systems far less inflationary than ours. The 19th century saw long *deflationary* periods in peacetime Europe: prices fell, real wages rose. Inflation, by contrast, is a subtle and devious attack on private property — destroying savings, hitting low earners hardest (a regressive tax). We will return to this.
- **Mercantilism** — the win-lose theory of trade — was indeed linked to monarchic competition, but also simply to the economic misunderstandings of the era.
- **Under democracy, the public domain has expanded tremendously** at the expense of private property, enabling the regulatory state through taxation and money-printing — and the timing matches the arrival of universal suffrage, as we will see in the next chapter.

### Power needs constraints — in its own interest

**Lord Acton**: "Power tends to corrupt, and absolute power corrupts absolutely. Great men are almost always bad men…" But the fuller story is **Mosca's**:

> "A ruling class that can get away with anything and can do anything in the name of a sovereign undergoes real moral degeneration… common to all men whose actions are exempt from the constraints and controls which the opinion and conscience of their fellow men usually impose."

The point is subtle and crucial: **it is in the ruling minority's own interest to be constrained** by public opinion. Unconstrained, it degenerates — and eventually loses power. This connects straight back to La Boétie: servitude is voluntary, people accept and even like being led, but the rule must remain tolerable. Tolerability is enforced by the majority's opinion and conscience — the constraint that keeps the minority healthy.

### The electoral function

Here is democracy's real advantage over authoritarian regimes: a successful democratic process lets the majority **publicly sanction or reward the different political forces within the ruling minority**. Voting is an official, public expression of public opinion. Mosca:

> "The electoral function is a means by which certain political forces control and limit the action of others — when it is exercised in good social conditions."

When you vote against the incumbents, you empower *other sections of the ruling minority* — shifting the balance so that the rule you live under becomes, hopefully, more tolerable. Democracy thereby **improves and speeds up the circulation of elites**.

**Ludwig von Mises** — who had surely read his Machiavellians — saw this as democracy's true social function:

> "Democracy is that form of political constitution which makes possible the adaptation of the government to the wishes of the governed without violent struggles."

No rebellions, no bloody revolutions: the pressure of majority dissatisfaction escapes through the valve of the ballot. For this reason Mosca described democracy as **relatively conservative**: functioning properly, it *protects* the ruling minority and the institutions from revolutionary toppling. Democracy has a kind of embedded conservatism.

### The catch

All of this works **only if conditions are right**. What are those conditions — and what happens when they stop being fulfilled? That question opens the next part of the course: the failure of democracy. But first, a look at modern democracies and the growth of the state, and at democracy's practical shortcomings.

## Modern Democracies and the Growth of the State

<chapterId>33e3c2fd-c0a4-48a2-8045-c3f82b172085</chapterId>

Modern democracies come in many constitutional flavors. For this course the differences matter little: what matters is that they are all **representative** democracies — and that they share the same developments and the same difficulties. Any type of democracy can work well or badly at a given period; it depends more on the leaders in power than on constitutional design. So let us look at what modern democratic systems have *in common*.

### The state grew with democracy

The growth of the state has gone hand in hand with the advent of democracy — specifically with **universal suffrage**. Before the 20th century, state spending in Western countries ran below 10% of GDP, often near 5%. Exactly when universal male suffrage arrived (late 19th–early 20th century), followed by women's suffrage, the modern state began its climb. *(Slide: table of suffrage dates per country + state-spending curve — insert from PPTX.)* This is not a coincidence; the mechanism unfolds below.

One enabler: careers in public service, once the domain of an aristocracy that served (and could hardly enrich itself in so small a state), became open to — and encouraged for — the average man. Society and the state began to *fuse*: the majority was invited into the state, and the state's interests started to resemble, or claim to resemble, society's.

### The monopoly problem

The state provides services to society; so do companies. Companies do it for profit, under competition — permanently forced to keep quality up and costs down. The state operates as a **monopoly**: services tend toward inefficiency, and bureaucracy tends to grow. Some nations manage the drift better than others (the Nordics, Germany), but no one escapes the rule.

### Did the people choose the welfare state?

Social protection expanded under democracy — assumed to be the will of the people. It is a tricky question:

- Yes, from the 1930s onward the majority kept voting for parties promising expanded welfare — naturally so.
- But over time, *all* parties converged on proposing such services. As noted earlier, after the Cold War the ideological divide collapsed; the voter's real choice narrowed. There is no longer a choice to change the *system*.
- And from the ruling minority's side, granting social services was a **tactical concession** — a way to appease the majority, keep public opinion favorable, and get on with its own affairs undisturbed.

For reference, the three broad welfare-state types — corporatist/conservative (Switzerland, Germany), mixed public-private (France, Spain — the private component is what lets very inefficient systems survive), and universalist social-democratic (the NHS, the Nordics; not means-tested) — matter less here than what they share.

Crises, when they come, tend to force a partial return to market solutions — Germany at the end of the 90s, Sweden and the Netherlands reforming their welfare states before the wall. Plato's recurrence again: things must become bad before they become better. France, having refused reform, is heading for the demonstration.

### Ten difficult developments

The modern democratic system is historically very young — a century, a century and a half. In that short time it has accumulated difficulties that are, unlike the wars, climate shocks and plagues of the past, **purely artificial**: caused by political decisions. Ten of them (not exhaustive):

1. **Generous welfare states + bloated bureaucracies** — inseparable twins, an increasing weight on society.
2. **Slowing growth and productivity** — technology masks it until it doesn't. Post-war growth owed much to a one-time rural-to-industrial transition, accompanied by high inflation and hyperactive Keynesian policy.
3. **Rising public debt and deficits** — France added a million civil servants in 25 years with no visible service improvement, digitalization notwithstanding.
4. **Persistent inflation** — wealth destruction and forced spending.
5. **Immigration and integration difficulties** — and a legitimate question: was this ever a democratic choice, or propaganda after the fact? *(Editorial flag.)*
6. **The constant dilemma** — the welfare state needs thriving market capitalism to fund it (the Nordics understood this best), yet must constantly expand the corporate and individual tax base as parties outbid each other's promises. Two mutually exclusive requirements.
7. **Spending priorities** — defense, subsidies to specific sectors and companies: never on any ballot. Are these priorities democratic?
8. **Security vs. liberty** — Hobbes' dilemma from *Leviathan*. Expanding state "security" (a conveniently fuzzy concept) means coercing individuals: military in US cities, expanding security apparatus in the EU. What remains of liberty?
9. **Ideological spending** — top-down public investment (foreign policy, climate, health) never agreed by the population that pays for it through taxes — and through debt, which is merely taxation of future generations.
10. **Cultural shift** — the deepest one. Nudging people into dependency on the state changes the culture: family structures disintegrate (the state prefers each individual bound to itself, not to family or community), fear of a stagnant future replaces ambition, nostalgia replaces forward-looking confidence (contrast Asia), and **risk aversion** spreads — fatal for a society that needs entrepreneurs. These shifts take a generation or two to undo.

### Growth happened *despite* the system

The standard riposte: "Look at the growth and technology modern democracy delivered!" The answer: economic growth and technological development happened **despite** modern democracy and the interventionist state, not because of them. With more economic freedom, growth would have been higher and adoption faster.

Are these developments merely coincidental with modern democracy, or *caused* by it? The claim of this course is: intimately related. But claiming is not enough — the analysis continues in the coming chapters, starting with democracy's practical shortcomings.

## Practical Shortcomings of Democracy

<chapterId>9560ad7e-61ed-4384-9ce0-4f385a21c23e</chapterId>

Beyond the political dynamics, democracy has *technical* shortcomings — mechanical properties of the system that make it less "people's rule" than advertised. They tend to be forgotten in grand debates about democracy and modernity; they never stop existing.

### The tyranny of the majority

**John Stuart Mill's** famous concept — and careful: this is a *different* majority/minority split than the Machiavellian one. Here the "minority" means people with outlying ideas — people like us, open to new ideas about money or democracy — and the "majority" means conventional opinion. In any large population, the lowest common denominator tends to prevail, and social pressure pushes everyone toward it. (Recall Timur Kuran: people lie in public and tell the truth in private — peer pressure in action. And yes, majority opinion is itself heavily shaped by media and the ruling minority — but however created, it presses on everyone.)

The political consequence: **centrism**. Voters prefer candidates and ideas of the center; outliers are filtered out; electoral choice narrows to what the conventional middle already believes.

### The death of ideology

Thirty years after the Cold War, the great left/right debate about statism versus non-statism seems over — reinforcing centrism. Few candidates, and programs that barely differ from one another. This is partly deliberate: a ruling elite has no interest in offering the people choices that could upend the existing social and political structure. Again: restricted choice.

### What about the 49%?

The question we should perhaps have asked first. If 51% elect a candidate who implements his program on *everyone* — what about the 49%? In plurality systems, counting non-voters, the unrepresented can exceed half the population. A system in which so large a portion gets no representation at all is hard to call "people's rule" — yet this is part and parcel of representative democracy, and rarely discussed.

**Gustave de Molinari** — the French-Belgian economist who was the first, in the 19th century, to propose that even security (police, courts, prisons) could be produced on the free market — put it sharply:

> "Democracy insists that the decisions of the majority must become law; that the minority is obliged to submit to it, even if it is contrary to its most deeply rooted convictions and injures its most precious interests."

A strong argument against democracy — at minimum, one every supporter of democracy should hold clearly in mind.

### Two structural theorems

- **Arrow's impossibility theorem**: there is never a clean, obvious social choice between A and B — the outcome depends on which alternatives are on the menu. Collective preference cannot be aggregated consistently.
- **Duverger's law**: in first-past-the-post systems (UK, US), the mechanics drive toward a **duopoly** — two parties alternating in power, their programs converging. Not a conspiracy; a natural consequence of how large populations vote under that rule. And relatively undemocratic in effect.

### Four feasible improvements

Interestingly, some fixes are simple and implementable — they would not solve the core problems, but they would improve democratic life:

1. **Expand parliaments.** Representation ratios have been shrinking: ever more citizens per elected official. Imagine tens of thousands of members of Congress, or thousands of French deputies — with remote-voting technology it is feasible — each representative closer and more accessible to fewer voters.
2. **Term limits and no accumulation of mandates** (mayor + senator + minister…): reduces corruption and nepotism, realigns the ruling minority with the ruled majority.
3. **Lower the parliamentary entry threshold.** Germany's and Sweden's 5% rules keep new parties and new ideas out — it is easy to see why the incumbents like it that way.
4. **End state funding of parties and media.** State support biases the field toward incumbents and starves new entrants.

Why are these obvious improvements not implemented? Because every one of them would **dilute the power of the ruling minority** — which is precisely why the ruling minority declines to implement them. The shortcomings of democracy are not all accidents.

This closes our tour of democracy's characteristics. Next part: why democracy fails — beginning with the limits of public opinion.

# The Failure of Democracy

<partId>f29213ab-f242-4980-81e6-0b86a180fcae</partId>


## The Limits of Public Opinion

<chapterId>7aeac33d-bf84-4cce-be67-8b6649ddf54e</chapterId>

We now enter the failure of democracy. We saw that democracy can serve as a valve, a conduit for public opinion — but public opinion has its limits, and those limits are one of democracy's key failure modes. The thesis of this chapter: **democracy is a victim of its own success — politically**. (The economic half of the story comes next.)

### Schmitt's conditions

**Carl Schmitt**, the legal theorist of interwar and authoritarian Germany, observed — correctly — that representative democracy *requires* discussion in the public sphere: openness, transparency, freedom of speech and of the press. Without those conditions, it slides toward authoritarianism. So far, uncontroversial: successful democracies are precisely the ones with transparency and free debate.

Combine that with what we established earlier: democracy works as a pressure valve — the majority expresses dissatisfaction with the ruling minority through elections — **only if conditions are right**. The conditions are Schmitt's: openness, transparency, public discussion.

### The self-undermining mechanism

Here is the problem. When those conditions are fulfilled, the resulting political scrutiny and healthy criticism inevitably turn *on the ruling minority itself*. Openness threatens its rule. So the ruling minority — which must and wants to continue ruling, while maintaining the *impression* of democracy — moves to stifle the very conditions democracy requires.

**Democracy, when it becomes too successful, undermines itself.** The values of democracy — openness, transparency, power to the ruled majority — are exactly contrary to the survival of the ruling minority. This is a recipe for failure, and it runs in cycles: conditions are right for a few decades, scrutiny builds, the minority throttles the openness, democracy is questioned, political crisis ensues. Plato's recurrence, again. The Western mistake is believing in an "end of history" where democracy simply continues forever, independent of everything else.

The trend is visible right now with **social media** — the first mass vehicle of political scrutiny in history: enormous open debate on X/Twitter and elsewhere, exactly the conditions democracy requires — and exactly what the ruling minority cannot accept. Hence the visible campaign to control and throttle it *(examples in transcript: TikTok's forced change of ownership, government pressure on Twitter and Facebook moderation — verify exact wording against video)*. This is not theory; it is happening as we speak.

### Stability vs. democratic form

Political stability is valued under every system — stability means predictability, investment, planning. And here is the uncomfortable lesson, straight from La Boétie: **stability comes from heeding public opinion, not from the democratic form**. A system is stable when it lets public opinion function as a conduit and allows the circulation of elites; the form of government is secondary. Western democracy currently delivers neither stability nor predictability — public frustration and instability now feed each other.

The counterexample is **China**. A Chinese academic close to the CCP writes that the government is stable and popular because it "tries to gauge the public's pulse in governance and reflect the public will." That is La Boétie's advice, applied by a communist dictatorship — popular *not* because it is democratic, but because it heeds opinion. And the same history shows the other side: the Great Leap Forward, the Cultural Revolution, the brutal COVID policies — decisions of a tiny elite, massively unpopular, costing the government real support. Mao's **Hundred Flowers campaign** (1956–57) even ran the full Schmitt cycle in miniature: "let a hundred flowers bloom, a hundred schools of thought contend" — openness encouraged, critique explodes, minority cannot bear it, crackdown. The West is living the same sequence in slow motion.

The lesson: it is never the *form* of the political system; it is whether a government, at a given moment, is wise enough to reflect the will of the people.

### The elite says it out loud

This is no longer a fringe diagnosis. In October 2025, Politico reported Chancellor **Merz** of Germany and President **Macron** of France warning that "Europe's democracies are in danger." Macron: Europe is undergoing "a degeneration of democracy due to attacks from within… we are turning on ourselves, we doubt our own democracy… something is happening to our democratic fabric; democratic debate is turning into a debate of hatred."

Read with Machiavellian eyes, this is the ruling minority expressing its fear at no longer controlling the ruled majority — at the moment in the cycle when only a circulation of the elite can restore stability. Even the ruling minority of Europe now recognizes, in its own vocabulary, exactly the mechanism described in this chapter.

Democracy undermines itself **politically**. Next: how it also undermines itself **economically** — the twin problem.

## The Twin Problem of Democracy

<chapterId>812d6123-539a-4c05-8453-3ec66f3fa472</chapterId>

Democracy undermines itself politically — that was the last chapter. This chapter completes the diagnosis: democracy also undermines itself **economically**. Together, these form the **twin problem of democracy**.

### The Mont Pelerin dilemma

The **Mont Pelerin Society** — the classical-liberal organization founded around Hayek in 1947 — gathered much of the European (later also American) economic elite. Its meeting minutes from the 1950s–70s have been published and studied, and a recurring concern leaps out: **democratic legislatures disrupt free markets**. These were men who *admired* democracy, wanted no authoritarian alternative — and yet found themselves asking a starkly undemocratic question: *how can the people be restricted*, so that free markets can function? Keep that tension in mind; the rest of the chapter explains why they felt it.

### Democracy as a tragedy of the commons

The **tragedy of the commons**: when no one owns the ocean, everyone overfishes — each fisherman gains today, and the depleted fishery ruins them all tomorrow. Short-term personal gain outweighs long-term collective ruin, so the ruin arrives.

Democracy, as the philosopher **Hans-Hermann Hoppe** argued, is exactly this. Voters support social and tax policies contrary to their own long-term interests: *I* (and my family) gain services now; the pressure on entrepreneurship, the slowed growth, the strangled economy come later, diffusely, to everyone — when they are visible at all. The majority — medium and low earners, under progressive taxation — reasons this way naturally, so center-left and center-right alike bid ever-higher welfare promises to win them. The long term has no constituency.

Then taxes hit their ceiling. Most of Europe is there now: raising them further breaks both voters and the economy — Sweden's ~90–100% marginal rates before its early-90s collapse being the textbook case (the **Laffer curve**: past a point, higher rates yield *less* revenue). What follows is a process of **de-civilization**, driven by the citizens' own votes: less consumption, less investment, fewer jobs, less to tax.

Two aggravating factors:

- **Immigration** *(editorial flag)* — new arrivals are net recipients for years (in Sweden, nine years on average before net contribution) and rationally vote for expanded welfare.
- **A global ruling minority.** Until the 1960s–70s, national elites kept some attachment to their local economies — a limit on how far they would let policy damage them. Today's internationally mobile, financially global elite has no such rootedness, hence even less incentive to resist locally destructive policies.

### Why bureaucracy makes it worse

- **Baumol effect**: public services grow costlier and less efficient over time.
- **Public choice theory** (James Buchanan, Nobel 1986): bureaucrats respond to incentives like everyone else — and a monopoly with no competitors, no lost bids, and no firings gives weak incentives for efficiency. Funds get allocated by connection and habit, not by what serves society; bureaucracies resist change and new technology.
- Bureaucracy stifles the economy three ways: (1) ever more rules and taxes; (2) state allocation of resources is far from optimal — the planner lacks the information the market price system carries (Mises, Hayek); (3) every bureaucrat is one person not producing in the private economy — France's six million state employees produce *something*, but far less than the same people would contribute on the market.

### Fiat money: democracy's financial engine

Welfare promises need financing beyond taxes, so democratic states accumulate **debt** — look at debt-to-GDP anywhere in the West. A permanently indebted state has a permanent interest in devaluing its debt, and the tool is **devaluing the currency**. Hence:

- **Fiat currency** — money with no intrinsic value, legal tender by government decree — paired with **centralized control of interest rates**, i.e. the price of money set by the state rather than the market.
- **The 2% "goal"** — central banks target 2% inflation as normal. Why not 1%? Why not zero? Compounded, 2% a year rapidly guts a currency: compare any price to 80 years ago. Goods did not become harder to produce; the money was debased.
- **Inflation properly defined** is the increase in the money supply: more money chasing the same goods. Price rises follow.
- **The Cantillon effect** (Richard Cantillon, early 18th century): new money benefits whoever touches it *first* — banks, the state, the ruling minority — who spend it before prices adjust. By the time it reaches the ruled majority, prices have risen. **Inflation is a regressive tax**: it hits hardest those furthest from the money spigot, and enriches those closest to it. This is not a side effect; it is a mechanism by which the ruling elite enriches itself at society's expense.
- **The wage-tax ratchet**: prices rise, wages follow with a lag, workers climb into higher tax brackets — paying more tax on unchanged purchasing power. The state wins again.
- The cultural cost: a debt-based, low-interest society encourages spending over saving, the present over the future.

**The solution is sound money**: decentralized money outside state control and limited in quantity — historically gold, today also **Bitcoin** (slowly increasing supply, hard cap). Plus **liberalization of interest rates**: market-set, free-floating rates that encourage saving and direct capital to the right sectors at the right time because they reflect the actual demand for money. We are far from this — but knowing the destination matters.

### The twin problem, stated

- **Democracy is not freedom** — it guarantees neither political nor economic liberty.
- **Democracy undermines itself politically** — driven by the **ruling minority**, which cannot tolerate the openness democracy requires (previous chapter).
- **Democracy undermines itself economically** — driven by the **ruled majority**, trapped in the tragedy of the commons (this chapter).

The responsibility for democracy's failure lies with *both* the minority and the majority. If it were only the elite's fault, the fix would be easy — swap the elite. It is not that easy: the problem is rooted in human nature. Which is why the way out requires **redefining democracy itself** — the subject of the next part.

# Real Democracy

<partId>f263ba00-f144-4952-a9b3-48a7a42004a9</partId>


## Political Self-Determination

<chapterId>0a918579-64f0-404e-82c8-8b90fd9ca03f</chapterId>

The diagnosis is complete: democracy undermines itself politically and economically. The only way out of this vicious circle is to **redefine democracy** — to bring it back toward, or identify it with, freedom itself. The redefinition has two sides: political (this chapter) and economic (the next).

### The false promise

Representative democracy promised self-government — and never ended the rule of the few. The fundamental tension between the promise (the majority rules) and the reality (a minority rules) is not shrinking; it is increasing, as the illusion dissolves. Social media has put long-suppressed ideas within everyone's reach, and the ruling minority's response — a forcefully, visibly pushed globalist agenda, and increasingly open restrictions on freedoms once taken for granted — reads as desperation. Meanwhile, as **Mises** wrote as early as 1919, *democracy is the best means for realizing socialism*: state growth, economic decline, political tension.

If representative democracy is neither freedom nor people's rule, can democracy be defined more suitably — closer to the actual meaning of the word?

### From majority rule to individual rule

Take democracy's stated goal seriously: the people rule. Not 51% ruling over 49% — that we have already rejected. Push the "majority" toward 100%: **everyone** should be able to realize their interests. But there is no "will of the majority", no Rousseauian general will — there are only **individual interests**, all slightly different, because in a developed society every individual is different.

So real democracy means: **each individual realizing his or her own interests** — politically and economically. Politically, that is **the right to self-determination**, understood in the widest possible sense. Mises again: democracy, rightly understood, is self-determination, self-government, self-rule — and *not* merely for delimited national units:

> …the right of "the inhabitants of every territory to decide on the state to which they wish to belong."

Down, ultimately, to the individual.

### Secession and subsidiarity

Taking self-determination seriously implies the **right to secede**: regions, towns, ultimately individuals choosing their own governance — independence from the centralized state, potentially as small independent states. History supports the idea: Germany and Italy spent centuries as constellations of small states, and were wealthy for it; studies consistently find that, all else equal, **smaller countries are freer, wealthier and happier** than large ones (compare Portugal, the Nordics, Luxembourg with France, Spain, Britain).

Corollaries: **subsidiarity** — laws as local as possible, decided and applied close to those they bind; and **decentralized money** — the euro is precisely the wrong direction; ideally even individual financial institutions could issue currency. Utopian-sounding, yes — the point is to understand the principles and the direction.

### The practical obstacles — honestly

Principles without practice are idle; the transition poses real problems that cannot be swept under the carpet:

- **Those who do not want to secede** — voluntary relocation? compensation? property rights across a secession, where properties change hands and owners exchange assets: complex, though not insoluble.
- **Population movements** — new borders trigger migrations in both directions, never to be forced, but to be anticipated, including their property dimension.
- **Resistance from the central state** — the hard one. No central government accepts losing territory:
  - **Legally**: most constitutions expressly forbid secession in the name of territorial unity (the US case being historically ambiguous — it took a civil war).
  - **Politically and militarily**: states suppress secession movements, sometimes violently — see Madrid's response to Catalonia's independence bid. (Except, tellingly, when outside powers *want* a secession: Kosovo, South Sudan.)
  - **Ideologically** — perhaps the strongest weapon: state education and propaganda teaching every child that the big country is great and indivisible. The longer a state has been unitary — France is the extreme case — the fewer of its people can even *imagine* secession.

Think in practice *and* in theory; neither works without the other. That is the political half of real democracy. The economic half — the free market — is next.

## The Free Market as Economic Democracy

<chapterId>281fc098-76d8-4083-ac2b-29d73c133a7e</chapterId>

The political side of real democracy is self-determination. This chapter completes the redefinition with the economic side: **the free market is the real economic democracy**.

### The right to choose — daily

Recall the redefinition: democracy as the realization of individual interests, for everyone, not for 51%. Economically, that means **the right to choose** — and not the impoverished choice of picking, once every four or five years, among a few candidates with near-identical programs. It means **choosing all the time**: on the marketplace, every day, in every exchange — to buy or not to buy, to associate or not to associate.

Contrast the two acts. The visit to the voting booth: rare, almost sacralized, and diluted into an ocean of ballots. The market choice: daily, direct, and always effective — *your* choice produces *your* outcome. **Mises** called this the **democracy of the market**: free markets enable genuine popular sovereignty, individuals voting continuously with their full revenue.

> "Capitalism is the consummation of consumers' self-determination." — Ludwig von Mises

### We do not live in a free market

An essential clarification. We are told from childhood that we live in free markets — and then told that "the free market fails" here and there. But what we live in is not a free market. A market where the state imposes heavy regulation and taxation, and controls the price of money itself (interest rates), is a **hampered market**. The **free market** is the *unhampered* one — where the state does not involve itself in the economic activity of companies, citizens and associations at all.

Even the standard measurements flatter us: indices like the Heritage Foundation's economic-freedom rankings score Western countries highly, but substantially understate the real economic coercion in Western democratic systems.

State intervention distorts the market twice over: it **empowers political elites** to steer society in directions the majority never chose, and it **introduces inefficiencies** — the growing bureaucracy, and taxes themselves, are pure additional costs.

### How far can the market go?

Even a minimal state — the **night-watchman state**, restricted to the regalian functions: courts, police, prisons, defense — keeps those services as monopolies, with the usual monopoly result: quality degrades, costs rise. (Recall Molinari, who already in the 19th century asked whether even security could be produced on the free market.) A completely stateless order is, from where we stand, extremely remote — the point here is simply that one can speak of a free market in *everything else* even while those regalian services remain state-run, and that the monopoly critique applies to them too.

### The two sides, together

**Real democracy = individual choice**, on both sides:

| | Political | Economic |
|---|---|---|
| Principle | Self-determination | Free-market exchange |
| Mechanism | Right of secession, decentralized governance, independence from coercive states | Voluntary, unforced exchange; no state interference |
| Rhythm | Communities choosing their state | Individuals choosing daily |

Frankly, one might prefer to drop the word "democracy" altogether and simply say **freedom** — but if we are going to keep discussing democracy, we should at least know what the real thing would be.

Yes, we are far from it — and in many respects moving in the wrong direction. But two lights in the darkness: education is improving (too slowly, and not always in the right domains, but improving); and the Greeks' recurrence still holds — nothing lasts forever, no statist order any more than a free one. State influence over society has reached a level that cannot continue; the transition may be bumpy, but it points toward more freedom. Which is also why the fight for liberty is constant, and liberty can never be taken for granted.

One question remains: concretely, **what is to be done?** The final chapter.

# What Is to Be Done

<partId>9c02b2c0-4693-4351-bb29-445eaff9f490</partId>


## What Is to Be Done

<chapterId>8fc53fe0-509c-4a68-a5b0-1b859c5e0a26</chapterId>

We know the destination: self-determination politically, the free market economically. We also know the terrain: a declining society in which the failure of democracy is in front of us daily. This final chapter looks at the road — the process as well as the end result — starting with a summary of the diagnosis.

### The problems of modern social democracy

"Social democracy" here means the democracy of expansive social services, high public debt and a large welfare state — the Western model this course has examined.

1. **It undermines itself politically.** Most people want to be ruled, and minority rule is always the case — a tension inherent in a system whose formula says the people rule. Democracy requires openness; openness endangers the ruling minority; the minority destroys the openness to stay in power. Hence the increasing coercion we see across the West.
2. **The ruling minority is disengaging.** More global than ever, less interested in local societies, and now openly interested in *restricting* democracy — in managing it — as economic conditions worsen. (Envy of China's financial leverage during the Great Recession was an early tell.)
3. **It undermines itself economically.** The tragedy of the commons: the majority votes itself short-term benefits at long-term collective cost. Be precise about responsibility: **the majority is fundamentally responsible for the economic disaster; the ruling minority for the political deficiencies.**
4. **It is inherently unstable** — and instability is a cost to everyone, under any system.
5. **One global elite.** The 19th century had several national ruling minorities; today there is essentially one, Anglo-Saxon-centered and financial, detached from every local society — Macron, more at ease hobnobbing abroad than engaging with French society, is the type specimen. The pressure-valve function of democracy barely works anymore, and the tension shows.

### The symptoms of decline

The consequences, visible now: economic stagnation and stifled innovation · inflation and fiat devaluation (together: stagflation, with fewer escape routes than the 1970s) · degrading infrastructure and public services (France, Italy, the US, Britain — and now even Sweden's trains and healthcare) · looming institutional bankruptcies (social security systems, the NHS) amid an increasingly unsustainable debt spiral · rising political coercion, shrinking freedom of dissent, politicians lying more as conditions worsen · collapsing trust in the political class on both sides of the Atlantic · rising crime and lawlessness *(editorial flag on the immigration passage)* · and the cultural toll: atomization of the family (the state prefers individuals bound to itself, not to each other), less saving, less human flourishing, a society that consumes the present because it fears the future.

All these elements are connected — one system, one source: trust placed in democracy, and liberty forgotten.

### Recognition first

Nothing can be done before the problem is recognized. Recognition is beginning, even in official quarters: France's own state website *vie-publique.fr* published (2023) a long analysis by a Bordeaux economics professor — "Rising public spending and impoverishment of public services: shedding light on the paradox." For readers of this course it is no paradox at all; that it appears on a state website is a sign.

The revolutionaries of 1789 opened the **Declaration of the Rights of Man** with the key sentence:

> "Ignorance, forgetfulness, or contempt of the rights of man are the sole causes of public misfortunes and of the corruption of governments."

**Ignorance** is the operative word. Five ignorances of today's majority — none of them the majority's *fault* (nobody taught them political economy), all of them addressable:

1. **Money** — almost nobody, however educated, understands the difference between sound money and fiat money. (This is where Plan B Network's mission connects directly.)
2. **Free trade** — opposed despite benefits established since the 18th century; watch tariff politics find mass support.
3. **Welfare** — supported despite the long-term harm; the tragedy of the commons unrecognized by its own participants.
4. **Interests** — the majority confuses its interests with the ruling minority's. When the media says "the United States wants…", it means Washington, not Americans. **Frederick Douglass** saw the pattern among his fellow slaves, who defended and took pride in their own masters. The mechanism has not changed.
5. **Cause and consequence** — we absolve the state for the decline its past actions caused, then ask the state to fix it. Confusing cause and consequence guarantees the cycle repeats.

**Thomas Jefferson**: "Enlighten the people generally, and tyranny and oppressions of body and mind will vanish like evil spirits at the dawn of day."

### The program — seven efforts

The details must be worked out locally, in each country and region. The general directions:

1. **Accept minority rule** — counterintuitive but essential. Minority rule is natural; the frustration of democracy comes from denying it. What matters is that leadership fall to **natural leaders**, emerged and accepted freely, rather than political leaders — and that their greater reward for greater responsibility be accepted as natural too.
2. **Liberalize state agencies and departments** — much of what the state runs need not be run by the state. The Nordic welfare reforms show it can be done; almost nowhere has it gone far enough.
3. **Empower decentralized alternatives** — local media, local governance, subsidiarity: no decision at national (or Brussels) level that can be taken locally.
4. **Reduce the state — dramatically.** Cut its income from both taxation and money-printing. A practical target: return to the 19th-century limited-government scale of roughly 5–10% of GDP. Not the ideal of the previous chapters, but a goal one can actually fight for.
5. **Defend property rights** — against the current laxism: judges who strictly protect property, taxes cut hard (taxation being a taking of property), and voluntary association upheld in its place.
6. **Discredit fiat money** — inflation is making people poorer, even where advancing technology masks it. Promote **sound money: gold, Bitcoin** — money no state can expand.
7. **Spread the truth** — the most important. Before anything else, a person must understand that the media does not tell the whole truth; nothing further is possible until that step. Then: information and education — about what democracy really is, and about freedom, its moral importance and its practical attractiveness. Lives genuinely improve in freedom, transition costs notwithstanding — and making freedom *attractive* is the key to the whole transformation.

### Closing

Return, finally, to Tocqueville: *"I dearly love liberty and respect for rights — but not democracy."* At the start of this course, that sentence read as a contradiction. Now it reads as a conclusion.
